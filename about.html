About

I am a Christian ethicist, PhD student, and former teacher who studies robots and the families who bring them home.

How I got here

“For I do not seek to understand that I may believe, but I believe in order to understand.”

Anselm of Canterbury, Proslogion 1. The book’s original title was Fides quaerens intellectum, faith seeking understanding.

I grew up in an old-school Pentecostal church. It taught me to seek the Spirit, but there wasn’t much room for hard questions. When I asked about God and Scripture as a young teenager, I got clichés, and eventually my doubts turned into atheism. If no one could answer a teenager’s questions, how could any of it be true?

A couple of years later I stumbled into apologetics, especially the work of William Lane Craig, and found answers I had never heard. That rekindled something, and at seventeen I felt called to help other people grow in their faith. So I became a theology major at Valley Forge, where biblical studies answered a lot of my old doubts and showed me how other traditions handled the same questions.

That pushed me to Pittsburgh Theological Seminary, a far more liberal place than where I started. They awarded me a scholarship for Christian leadership anyway, and I spent two years arguing in real time with views I didn’t hold. I kept my Pentecostal roots, but I came out with a more ecumenical faith and a conviction that intellectual honesty belongs in the church.

After seminary I spent five years working in mental health, first in behavioral health and then as a case manager. I worked alongside children who were struggling and helped build stronger care networks around them.

Since then I’ve written curriculum for youth and small groups, spent five years teaching Bible, Theology, Christian Ethics, Research, and Psychology at a Christian school, and started a PhD in public theology. The thread through all of it is the same. I want people with real questions to get real answers instead of clichés.
