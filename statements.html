This page has three short statements: how I research with digital tools, how I teach with them, and how all of it ties into my calling as a public theologian.

I. Research II. Teaching III. Public theology

About this portfolio

I study companion robots, the kind built to keep people company. A lot of them are sold to parents for their kids or to families caring for someone. Many of these robots also record what happens around them, and the person who grows attached to one usually isn’t the person who agreed to the terms. So my question is pretty simple: what should families, caregivers, and churches know before bringing one home?

This portfolio collects my work from HT 930, Digital and Public Humanities, at United Lutheran Seminary in the fall of 2026. Below are my statements on research, teaching, and public theology. Work has what I made this semester.

I.

Research statement

Frameworks
Theological anthropology, the Imago Dei, Value Sensitive Design

Methods
Dublin Core, FAST subject terms, Wayback Machine, archive.today

My research is on the ethics of physical and embodied AI in care settings, and my dissertation is still in progress.

Companion robots are companions, but they’re also collecting data, and sometimes they work as surveillance tools. The person who gets attached to the robot usually isn’t the person who agreed to its terms of service. I call this architectural asymmetry.

I work from the primary sources themselves. I read the privacy policies, app store data-safety disclosures, product documentation, and what developers can access through the SDK, instead of relying on someone else’s summary of what a device does. On the theological side, I lean on theological anthropology and the Imago Dei. For design ethics, I use Value Sensitive Design from Batya Friedman and David Hendry.

This semester I built a public archive, Who Agreed?, around the documents that govern ten companion robots. For each robot, I archived the document that governs its data and described it with all fifteen Dublin Core elements. I pulled subject terms from FAST so they match the terms libraries already use. Companies change their policies without notice, so every record links to a dated copy on the Wayback Machine or archive.today.

II.

Teaching statement

In the classroom
Structured debate, Wesley’s quadrilateral

Sources students used
New Advent, Bible Hub, Bible Study Tools, JSTOR, EBSCOhost

When I teach something contested, I put students inside the argument. In Old Testament Ethics, I used a structured debate where students had to argue a position they didn’t hold and then answer the strongest objection to it. I wasn’t trying to settle the question in one class period. I wanted them to learn how to disagree well, which sticks with them long after the details fade.

Designing curriculum for grades six through twelve taught me to see each course as one piece of a longer path. What a ninth grader can do with a primary source depends on what they learned years earlier, so I plan backward from what I want a graduating senior to be able to do.

My convictions about technology come into the classroom with me. Tools should support a student’s judgment, and AI can help refine work a student has already done, but I don’t want it doing the work for them. I’d rather read something rough that’s genuinely theirs than something polished that isn’t. And before we decide which tool to use, we have to talk about what that tool is allowed to do and who decided that.

In a research class, I’d start with a real-time demo. I’d ask ChatGPT or Claude a research question right in front of the students, and then we’d check its answer against the actual primary and secondary documentation. That shows them where hallucinations or faulty information can seep through the cracks. From there, I’d teach them how to use AI well: to gather primary and secondary sources, brainstorm and ideate, build frameworks and outlines, test their ideas, and help with peer review.

I’d also teach students to network map. It gives them a better understanding of the “who” behind the “what” they’re studying, and of whose lives are touched, directly or indirectly, by the subject matter.

I also want students to be able to explain their work to someone outside the class. If it only makes sense in seminar vocabulary, it isn’t done yet.

In my own classes, I put real sources in front of students and taught them how to use them. For primary texts they worked in New Advent, Bible Hub, and Bible Study Tools. In theology, I used Wesley’s theological quadrilateral of Scripture, tradition, reason, and experience as a starting point, and students worked outward from it into the commentaries. For research, they used JSTOR and EBSCOhost, and they used AI to help gather sources and test their ideas, not to do the writing for them.

Some tech is more prone to gather people than others, so part of teaching with it is learning to tell the difference. Some tools naturally lend themselves to in-groups and out-groups, like online sub-forums, while others are open and collaborative. I want my students and small groups using the second kind.

III.

Digital humanities and public theology

Written for
Chaplains, families weighing care decisions, and the staff who do the daily work of care

Public theology is at the center of this work for me. The people who most need it, like chaplains, families weighing care decisions, and the staff who do the daily work of care, aren’t reading robotics ethics journals. So writing for them is part of the research itself.

Since this project lives in public theology, I want readers to think about companion bots in light of two ideas that are both anthropological and theological. First, the relationships we build shape us as humans, no matter who or what we’re relating to. Second, physical AI makes embodied relationships with technology possible, which could move us from individualized, disembodied tech back toward more communal, embodied ways of living.

I feel most accountable to the wider public, because this technology has a direct pipeline into people’s homes through capitalist markets. For me, that means making knowledge accessible without sacrificing excellent research, and being transparent about my methods and biases.
